## Introduction

Long words are one of the simplest reasons non-fiction often feels slower to read than fiction. The effect is not dramatic on any single word. Most skilled readers recognise long words quickly. The slowdown appears because hundreds of slightly longer recognition events accumulate across a page. A report filled with terms such as “administrative”, “constitutional”, “pharmacological”, or “macroeconomic” demands more visual and linguistic processing than a page built mainly from short, familiar words. Research on reading rates, [eye movements]({{ 'eye-tradeoff/' | relative_url }}), and text difficulty consistently shows that word length influences how quickly readers move through text and helps explain why non-fiction is often read more slowly than narrative prose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17470218211017100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals+2Gwern]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsImproving reading rate prediction with word length...28 Apr 2021 — The equation predicts that Dutch texts with an average w...</span></span></span>

## Why Word Length Affects Recognition Speed

Reading is not a process of taking in an entire line at once. The eyes pause briefly on words, gather information, and then jump forward. Word length influences both where the eyes land and how long they stay.

[Eye-tracking]({{ 'eye-tracking/' | relative_url }}) studies show that longer words are less likely to be skipped and more likely to receive longer fixations than short words. Readers also use information about word length when planning eye movements, meaning that longer words affect the mechanics of reading before they are even fully recognised.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6001944/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC+2PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of Word Length on Eye Guidance Differ for Young and...by S Li · 2018 · Cited by 42 — Specifically, longer words are more like...</span></span></span>

This happens for several reasons:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Longer words contain more letters that must be visually processed.
* They often require more than one fixation to identify accurately.
* They are statistically less common than short everyday words.
* They frequently contain prefixes, suffixes, or specialised structures that require additional analysis.

</div>

The result is not a major delay on any single encounter. A skilled adult may spend only a fraction of a second longer processing a longer word. However, when dozens of such words appear on every page, those fractions accumulate into a noticeable reduction in overall reading speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2715992/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by A Pollatsek · 2008 · Cited by 128 — The frequency and length of a word both influence how long people fixate it when silently readi...</span></span></span>

## How Technical Vocabulary Accumulates Processing Cost

Word length matters most in non-fiction because long words are often tied to specialised [vocabulary]({{ 'knowledge/' | relative_url }}).

A novel might repeatedly use short, highly familiar words such as “house”, “walk”, “friend”, “door”, or “look”. By contrast, a textbook, research article, policy document, or business report may rely on terms such as “implementation”, “correlation”, “infrastructure”, “methodology”, or “jurisdiction”. These words are not only longer; they are often encountered less frequently in everyday life.

Research on eye movements shows that both word length and word frequency influence fixation time. A long word that is also relatively uncommon typically creates a larger processing burden than a short, highly familiar one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2715992/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by A Pollatsek · 2008 · Cited by 128 — The frequency and length of a word both influence how long people fixate it when silently readi...</span></span></span>

This interaction helps explain why some non-fiction passages feel unexpectedly dense. Consider a sentence describing a character entering a room compared with a sentence explaining organisational restructuring. The second sentence may contain multiple long abstract nouns and [technical]({{ 'technical-texts/' | relative_url }}) modifiers. Even when the concepts are understood, readers must invest slightly more effort in recognising and integrating each term.

Across an entire chapter, that extra effort becomes measurable. Studies that model reading speed have found that average word length significantly improves predictions of how quickly a text will be read. In other words, knowing the typical length of words in a passage provides useful information about its expected reading rate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17470218211017100" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: journals.sagepub.com">[Sage Journals]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">journals.sagepub.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sage JournalsImproving reading rate prediction with word length...28 Apr 2021 — The equation predicts that Dutch texts with an average w...</span></span></span>

## Why the Slowdown Feels Larger Than It Is

Readers often overestimate how much individual long words affect them. The real mechanism is cumulative rather than dramatic.

Imagine a page containing 500 words. If many of those words require slightly longer fixations and are skipped less often, the reader may spend several additional seconds on that page without consciously noticing any single obstacle. The reading experience simply feels slower and more effortful.

This accumulation effect aligns with broader reading-rate research. Reviews of adult reading speed show that non-fiction is typically read more slowly than fiction, and differences in vocabulary characteristics, including word length, account for a meaningful portion of that gap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/linguistics/2019-brysbaert.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gwern.net">[Gwern+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gwern.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How many words do we read per minute?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A review and...For silent reading of English non-fiction most adults fall in the range of 175–300 wpm; for fiction the range is. 200–320...</span></span></span>

Importantly, this does not imply poorer reading ability. A slower pace can be a normal response to the language on the page. The text itself contributes to reading speed.

## When Shorter Non-Fiction Can Read Quickly

Not all non-fiction is slowed by long words. Many forms of non-fiction are written with deliberately simple vocabulary and can be read at speeds comparable to fiction.

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Well-written newspaper articles.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10360968/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by M Brysbaert · 2023 · Cited by 12 — These are the difficulty levels seen in newspaper articles. Average word length was 5.3 letters...</span></span></span>
* Popular science books aimed at general audiences.
* Clear instructional material.
* Magazine features.
* Introductory educational content.

</div>

When authors replace specialised terminology with shorter, familiar alternatives, readers spend less time recognising words and more time following ideas. The result is a smoother reading experience even when the subject matter remains informative.

This is one reason readability guidelines often encourage plain language. ## What This Means for Increasing Reading Speed

For adults seeking to increase reading speed, word length provides an important reminder: reading rate depends partly on the text, not only on the reader.

A person who reads a novel at 280 words per minute may naturally slow when reading a policy paper packed with long technical terms. That reduction is not necessarily a sign of weaker skill. It reflects the additional recognition work required by the language itself.

Because long words are more likely to attract fixations, resist skipping, and require longer processing times, they create a small but persistent drag on reading speed. In non-fiction, where specialised vocabulary appears repeatedly, those costs accumulate page after page, helping explain why the same reader can move comfortably through fiction yet feel noticeably slower when tackling dense informational prose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6001944/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov">[Sage Journals+3PMC+3PMC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Effects of Word Length on Eye Guidance Differ for Young and...by S Li · 2018 · Cited by 42 — Specifically, longer words are more like...</span></span></span>
