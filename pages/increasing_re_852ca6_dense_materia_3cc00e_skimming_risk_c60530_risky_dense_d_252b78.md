## Introduction

[Increasing]({{ 'increasing/' | relative_url }}) reading speed is useful when the goal is orientation, but some documents are too costly to misunderstand. Legal contracts, medical instructions, prescribing information, eligibility criteria, and technical compliance documents often place critical limitations in short phrases that are easy to skip. In these contexts, the risk is not merely missing a detail. A missed qualifier can change who is covered, who is liable, who is eligible for treatment, or whether a medicine can be used safely. The challenge is that the most important information is often buried inside exceptions, conditions, and [definitions]({{ 'definitions/' | relative_url }}) rather than highlighted in the main message. When the consequences involve money, safety, compliance, or health, skimming shifts from a speed technique to a risk-management decision. ## Why Contracts, Guidelines, and Specifications Punish Missed Details

Many readers assume that the main point of a document appears in its headings or opening paragraphs. Legal and medical documents frequently work differently. The broad rule may appear first, while the practical limits appear later.

In contracts, rights and obligations are often narrowed by [exceptions]({{ 'exceptions/' | relative_url }}) introduced through words such as “unless”, “except”, “subject to”, or “provided that”. A reader who remembers the general promise but misses the exception may walk away with the opposite understanding of what the agreement actually requires. Legal scholars and practitioners consistently note that contract interpretation can hinge on precise wording, grammar, and ambiguity rather than broad intent.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/in-house/do-not-forget-your-commas-the-importance-of-language-and-grammar-in-contract-drafting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lawsociety.org.uk">[Law Society+2Stanford Law School]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lawsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Law SocietyWhy clear language makes for strong contracts26 Jul 2023 — Daniela Licciardo explains why grammar and language are so importan...</span></span></span>

Medical documents present a similar problem. A treatment recommendation may apply only to a specific patient group, age range, medical history, or risk category. Regulatory agencies devote entire sections of prescribing information to contraindications, warnings, precautions, [interactions]({{ 'interactions/' | relative_url }}), and use in special populations because seemingly small distinctions can have major safety implications. U.S. Food and Drug Administration+2U.S. Food and Drug Administration<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/warnings-and-precautions-contraindications-and-boxed-warning-sections-labeling-human-prescription" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fda.gov">[fda.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fda.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Food and Drug AdministrationWarnings and Precautions, Contraindications, and Boxed...May 5, 2020 — This guidance is intended to assist a...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 5, 2020</span></span></span>

[Technical]({{ 'technical-texts/' | relative_url }}) specifications and compliance documents create another version of the same risk. A requirement that appears mandatory may apply only under certain operating conditions. Missing those conditions can lead to regulatory failures, procurement mistakes, or implementation errors.

The common pattern is that dense documents concentrate risk in a few clauses rather than distributing it evenly across the text.

## How Nested Clauses and Narrow Eligibility Rules Change Outcomes

The danger of [skimming]({{ 'skimming/' | relative_url }}) is not simply that information is missed. It is that the missed information often controls the interpretation of everything around it.

### Eligibility Rules That Look Broader Than They Are

Medical guidance frequently uses layered eligibility criteria. For example, contraceptive guidance from health authorities does not merely state whether a method is recommended. Recommendations change according to specific medical conditions, age groups, and risk factors. The difference between “generally appropriate” and “not recommended for patients with this condition” may be contained in a table entry or footnote rather than a headline statement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549158" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: who.int">[World Health Organization+2CoSRH]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">who.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Health Organization Medical eligibility criteria for contraceptive use</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Health OrganizationMedical eligibility criteria for contraceptive useFebruary 3, 2015 — 3 Feb 2015 — This document is part of the p...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 3, 2015</span></span></span>

A fast reader may retain the broad recommendation while overlooking the condition that determines whether it applies to a particular person.

### Contraindications and Drug Warnings

Prescription drug information contains sections specifically designed to prevent dangerous oversights. Contraindications identify situations in which a drug should not be used. Warnings and precautions describe serious risks, and boxed warnings represent the strongest safety warnings required by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. These warnings exist because the consequences of overlooking a narrow restriction can include severe injury, hospitalisation, or death. ### Contract Exceptions That Reverse the Rule

Contracts often contain a pattern that appears straightforward until a later clause narrows it. A service provider may appear to guarantee performance, but a later limitation-of-liability clause may sharply restrict remedies. A refund policy may seem generous until exclusions are introduced. A confidentiality obligation may appear broad until carve-outs define information that is not protected.

Research and legal commentary on contracts repeatedly emphasise that disputes frequently arise from wording, ambiguity, and interpretation of specific clauses rather than disagreement about the document's overall purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lawsociety.org.uk/topics/in-house/do-not-forget-your-commas-the-importance-of-language-and-grammar-in-contract-drafting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lawsociety.org.uk">[Law Society+2arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lawsociety.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Law SocietyWhy clear language makes for strong contracts26 Jul 2023 — Daniela Licciardo explains why grammar and language are so importan...</span></span></span>

The faster the reading speed, the greater the chance that a reader remembers the rule and misses the carve-out.

## Why Familiarity Creates False Confidence

One reason legal and medical documents are particularly risky to skim is that they often look familiar.

Most people have seen contracts before. Most people have read medicine labels, consent forms, insurance documents, or workplace policies. Familiarity encourages prediction. Readers assume that a new document says roughly what previous documents said.

This assumption can be costly because high-stakes documents are often differentiated precisely by the details that seem least noticeable. A changed dosage instruction, a revised warning, a new exclusion, or a different eligibility criterion may be the only reason the document exists.

Research and regulatory practice reflect this reality. Drug labels are routinely updated with new warnings, contraindications, and safety information as evidence changes. In one review of FDA safety actions, regulators added numerous new boxed warnings, contraindications, and other safety warnings in a single year, illustrating how critical information can evolve over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/1108624" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jamanetwork.com">[JAMA Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jamanetwork.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JAMA NetworkThe FDA and New Safety Warningsby TJ Moore · 2012 · Cited by 56 — In 2009, the FDA approved 181 major safety regulatory actio...</span></span></span>

Readers who rely on memory from previous versions may overlook exactly the information that changed.

## A Practical Risk Test Before Deciding to Skim

Before using a [speed-reading]({{ 'myths/' | relative_url }}) approach on a dense document, ask three questions.

**What is the cost of being wrong?**

If misunderstanding the document could affect health, legal rights, compliance obligations, employment status, insurance coverage, or significant financial decisions, the document should be treated as high risk.

**Does the document contain eligibility rules or exceptions?**

Documents built around conditions, exclusions, thresholds, contraindications, exemptions, or special cases deserve careful reading. These structures place crucial meaning in small textual details. World Health Organization+2U.S. Food and Drug Administration<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241549158" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: who.int">[who.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">who.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Health Organization Medical eligibility criteria for contraceptive use</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Health OrganizationMedical eligibility criteria for contraceptive useFebruary 3, 2015 — 3 Feb 2015 — This document is part of the p...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 3, 2015</span></span></span>

**Am I looking for orientation or accuracy?**

Skimming is effective when deciding whether a document is relevant or locating sections that deserve attention. It becomes unsafe when the goal is to determine exactly what is permitted, required, recommended, covered, or prohibited.

A useful compromise is a two-pass approach:

1. Skim first to understand structure and identify critical sections.
2. Read slowly wherever you encounter definitions, eligibility criteria, warnings, exclusions, exceptions, dosage instructions, liability limitations, or compliance requirements.

This preserves much of the speed benefit while reducing the chance of missing the few sentences that carry most of the document's practical consequences.

* Medical consent forms.
* Treatment eligibility guidelines.
