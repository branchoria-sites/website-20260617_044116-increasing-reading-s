## Introduction

The famous “baseball study” is often cited as proof that [background]({{ 'expertise/' | relative_url }}) knowledge can outweigh reading skill. That interpretation contains an important truth, but it also stretches the original findings further than the researchers intended. For anyone interested in [increasing]({{ 'increasing/' | relative_url }}) reading speed, the study is valuable because it shows how familiar knowledge can reduce the effort required to understand a text. At the same time, it does not prove that reading skill is unimportant, nor does it show that subject knowledge alone can replace broader literacy development. Understanding both the finding and its limits helps readers draw the right lesson from one of the most frequently discussed studies in reading research.

## What the study tested

In the late 1980s, researchers Donna Recht and Lauren Leslie investigated how prior [knowledge]({{ 'knowledge/' | relative_url }}) affects comprehension. They recruited 64 students in Years 8 and 9 (roughly equivalent to seventh and eighth grade in the United States), assessed both their reading ability and their knowledge of baseball, and divided them into groups accordingly. The students then read a [short passage]({{ 'passage-length-0559bd/' | relative_url }}) describing a baseball inning and were asked to reconstruct what happened using figures on a model baseball field.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yesataretelearningtrust.net/Portals/0/Effect-of-Prior-Knowledge-on-Good-and-Poor-Readers-Memory-of-Text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yesataretelearningtrust.net">[Yes @ Areté Learning Trust+2Great Minds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yesataretelearningtrust.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Effect of Prior Knowledge on Good and Poor Readers Memory of Text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yes @ Areté Learning TrustEffect of Prior Knowledge on Good and Poor Readers&#x27;...by DR Recht · 1988 · Cited by 827 — We investigated how...</span></span></span>

The design was notable because it separated two factors that are often tangled together:

* General reading ability.
* Specific knowledge of the topic being read.

The result that attracted attention was that students with strong baseball knowledge often understood the passage better than students who were considered stronger readers but knew little about baseball. In some cases, weaker readers with high baseball knowledge outperformed stronger readers with low baseball knowledge. ## Why subject knowledge improved recall

The baseball passage was filled with information that would have been easy to organise if a reader already understood the game. Concepts such as innings, runners, outs and field positions provided a ready-made framework for interpreting events.

Researchers and cognitive psychologists often describe this as a knowledge structure or mental framework. When readers possess relevant knowledge, they do not have to work out every relationship from scratch. Instead, new information can be attached to existing understanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aft.org/ae/spring2006/willingham" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aft.org">[American Federation of Teachers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aft.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">By Daniel T. Willingham. &quot;Knowledge is Good.&quot; So read the motto of the mythical Faber...Read more...</span></span></span>

For reading speed, this matters because comprehension becomes more efficient in several ways:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Fewer pauses for interpretation.** Familiar concepts are recognised quickly.
* **Better prediction.** Readers can anticipate likely events and relationships.
* **Stronger memory.** New details fit into an existing mental model.
* **Reduced cognitive load.** Less working memory is spent figuring out basic context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aft.org/ae/spring2006/willingham" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aft.org">[American Federation of Teachers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aft.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">By Daniel T. The baseball study therefore supports an important idea about fluent reading: knowledge can make text feel easier and faster because less mental effort is required to construct meaning.

## Why later critics urge caution

The study's influence has led some commentators to use it as evidence for claims that go beyond the actual experiment. Several criticisms recur in later discussions.

### It was a single study with a specific text

The experiment involved one relatively short passage about one topic with a modest sample of students. Critics note that the dramatic contrast between knowledge and reading ability has not been reproduced through a large body of identical studies using many different topics and texts. The study remains influential, but it is still one piece of evidence rather than a final answer.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/prior-knowledge-or-he-isnt-going-to-pick-on-the-baseball-study" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shanahanonliteracy.com">[Shanahan on Literacy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shanahanonliteracy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">prior knowledge or he isnt going to pick on the baseball study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shanahan on LiteracyPrior Knowledge, Or He Isn&#x27;t Going to Pick on the Baseball...14 Mar 2020 — This blog entry explores what is known ab...</span></span></span>

### It did not compare teaching methods

A common misunderstanding is that the study proved content knowledge should replace reading instruction. In reality, the researchers did not compare different educational approaches. They measured existing knowledge and existing reading ability. The study therefore cannot tell us whether teaching more background knowledge is better than teaching reading strategies, decoding, vocabulary or any other instructional method.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nepc.colorado.edu/blog/baseball-study" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nepc.colorado.edu">[National Education Policy Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nepc.colorado.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Education Policy Center Alfie Kohn: The *&amp;%$!#!</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Baseball Study: Why Are Fans of Fact...Apr 8, 2025 — It just looked at students&#x27; prior reading scores and the knowledge of the topic in...</span></span></span>

### It measured comprehension in a knowledge-rich situation

The passage was deliberately chosen because baseball knowledge was expected to vary among students. That makes the study excellent for demonstrating the importance of prior knowledge. However, it does not show what happens across all forms of reading, especially when readers encounter unfamiliar subjects and must learn [new concepts]({{ 'new-concepts/' | relative_url }}) from the text itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.yesataretelearningtrust.net/Portals/0/Effect-of-Prior-Knowledge-on-Good-and-Poor-Readers-Memory-of-Text.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: yesataretelearningtrust.net">[Yes @ Areté Learning Trust]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">yesataretelearningtrust.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Effect of Prior Knowledge on Good and Poor Readers Memory of Text</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Yes @ Areté Learning TrustEffect of Prior Knowledge on Good and Poor Readers&#x27;...by DR Recht · 1988 · Cited by 827 — We investigated how...</span></span></span>

### Knowledge can help and mislead

Research reviews have found that background knowledge generally supports comprehension, but existing knowledge can sometimes create misunderstandings if readers bring incorrect assumptions to a text. Prior knowledge is not automatically beneficial; its effect depends partly on whether that knowledge is accurate and relevant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02702711.2021.1888348" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tandfonline.com">[Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tandfonline.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A critical review was conducted to determine the influence background knowledge has on the reading comprehension of primary school-aged c...</span></span></span>

## What the baseball study means for reading speed

The strongest takeaway is narrower than many popular summaries suggest. The baseball study demonstrates that relevant knowledge can dramatically improve comprehension of a text about that subject. Readers who already possess useful knowledge often process information more quickly, remember and experience less strain while reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aft.org/ae/summer2020/wexler" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aft.org">[American Federation of Teachers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aft.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Recht and Leslie chose baseball...</span></span></span>

For increasing reading speed, the practical lesson is not “knowledge beats reading skill”. It is that reading speed and comprehension are partly built outside the reading session itself. The more vocabulary, concepts and subject familiarity a reader brings to a text, the less work is required to understand each sentence.

At the same time, the study does not justify ignoring broader reading skills. Skilled readers still need decoding, vocabulary knowledge, inference-making ability and strategies for approaching unfamiliar material. The baseball experiment shows the power of background knowledge, but it does not show that background knowledge is the only ingredient that matters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.shanahanonliteracy.com/blog/prior-knowledge-or-he-isnt-going-to-pick-on-the-baseball-study" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: shanahanonliteracy.com">[Shanahan on Literacy+2Taylor &amp; Francis Online]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">shanahanonliteracy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">prior knowledge or he isnt going to pick on the baseball study</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Shanahan on LiteracyPrior Knowledge, Or He Isn&#x27;t Going to Pick on the Baseball...14 Mar 2020 — This blog entry explores what is known ab...</span></span></span>

Viewed in that light, the study remains a valuable reminder that one of the most reliable routes to faster reading is often indirect: learn more about a subject, and texts in that subject become easier, quicker and more meaningful to read.
