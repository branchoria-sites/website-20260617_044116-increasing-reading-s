## Introduction

Phrase-cued reading is a simple but powerful way to help readers move from word-by-word decoding to smooth, expressive reading. In a phrase-[cued text]({{ 'cued-text/' | relative_url }}), natural [word groups]({{ 'word-groups/' | relative_url }}) are marked with slashes, spacing, or visual “scoops” that show where ideas belong together. Instead of reading every word as a separate unit, readers learn to process meaningful chunks of language. Research on fluency consistently links this kind of phrasing to stronger prosody—the rhythm, expression, and meaningful pausing that make reading sound natural—as well as improved comprehension.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED313689" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eric.ed.gov">[ERIC+2NASET]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by TV Rasinski · 1990 · Cited by 16 — Rasinski, Timothy V. ## Can marked phrases make reading smoother?

Yes—particularly for readers who can decode words but still sound choppy or robotic when reading aloud.

Fluent readers naturally group words into meaningful units. For example:

*The boy with the red backpack / hurried across the playground.*

A struggling reader may pause after nearly every word. A fluent reader recognises that *the boy with the red backpack* functions as a single idea and reads it as one unit. Phrase-cued text makes these groupings visible until the reader begins to recognise them independently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED313689" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eric.ed.gov">[ERIC+2interventioncentral.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by TV Rasinski · 1990 · Cited by 16 — Rasinski, Timothy V. As in processing oral speech, proficient reading involves &quot;chunking&quot; writt...</span></span></span>

Research associated with Timothy Rasinski's work on phrase boundaries found that marking natural phrase breaks can improve reading performance and comprehension, especially for readers who have difficulty recognising sentence structure while reading.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED313689" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by TV Rasinski · 1990 · Cited by 16 — Rasinski, Timothy V. The goal is not to teach readers to pause at every mark forever. The marks act as temporary scaffolding, helping readers hear and feel how fluent reading should sound.

## What phrase-cued text looks like

Phrase-cued text usually contains visual markers that separate meaningful language units.

A sentence might appear as:

*After the heavy rain / the river rose quickly / and flooded nearby fields.*

The markings are based on syntax and meaning rather than arbitrary word counts. Readers are encouraged to read each phrase smoothly before moving to the next.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED313689" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">by TV Rasinski · 1990 · Cited by 16 — Rasinski, Timothy V. ## How cues support prosody and comprehension

### Expression grows from meaningful phrasing

Prosody is often described as reading with appropriate expression, phrasing, stress, and intonation. Fluent readers do not simply read quickly; they read in ways that reflect meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2024.1440760/full" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: frontiersin.org">[Frontiers+2Reading Rockets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">frontiersin.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FrontiersImproving automated scoring of prosody in oral reading...by K Wang · 2024 · Cited by 7 — Prosody is one dimension of oral readi...</span></span></span>

Phrase cues help because expression becomes easier when readers know which words belong together. Consider the difference between:

*The old man / said the teacher.*

and

*The old man said / the teacher.*

The same words can communicate different meanings depending on phrasing. Learning to group words correctly supports both understanding and expression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.interventioncentral.org/academic-interventions/reading-comprehension/phrase-cued-text-lessons" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: interventioncentral.org">[interventioncentral.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">interventioncentral.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Phrase-Cued Text LessonsBecause phrases are units that often encapsulate key ideas, the student&#x27;s ability to identify them can enhance co...</span></span></span>

As readers gain confidence with phrase boundaries, their reading typically sounds less mechanical and more like spoken language. This development is important because fluent reading is characterised not only by accuracy and speed but also by natural expression.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.readingrockets.org/reading-101/reading-101-learning-modules/course-modules/fluency" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: readingrockets.org">[Reading Rockets+2Reading Rockets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">readingrockets.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Reading Rockets Fluency</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reading RocketsFluency: IntroductionFluent readers read aloud effortlessly and with expression — their reading sounds natural, as if they...</span></span></span>

### Better phrasing reduces cognitive load

When readers process language in larger chunks, they spend less effort assembling meaning one word at a time. This frees mental resources for comprehension. ## When to fade support so readers phrase independently

Phrase cues are intended as temporary guidance, not a permanent feature of every text.

A practical progression often looks like this:

1. If cue marks remain indefinitely, readers may become dependent on them. The long-term objective is for readers to recognise phrase boundaries automatically as they encounter new texts.

## Why phrase cues matter for reading speed

Phrase-cued reading is often discussed as a prosody strategy, but it also contributes to faster reading. Fluent readers process larger units of language at once rather than stopping after each word. As phrasing becomes more automatic, reading becomes more efficient and less effortful. [Reading Rockets+2PMC](https://www.readingrockets.org/reading-101/reading-101-learning-modules/course-modules/fluency)

The most important outcome is not speed alone. Effective phrase cueing helps readers reach a point where speed, comprehension, and expression work together. When natural phrase breaks become internalised, reading sounds smoother, meaning is easier to follow, and the path toward greater reading speed becomes more natural and sustainable.
