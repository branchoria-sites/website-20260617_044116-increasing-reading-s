## Introduction

A reading-speed test is easy to game. Someone can race through a passage, remember a handful of isolated facts, and post an impressive words-per-minute score while missing the argument, the relationships between ideas, or the author's conclusion. That is why the quality of the comprehension quiz matters as much as the [timing]({{ 'timing/' | relative_url }}) itself.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/increasing_re_852ca6_reading_speed_ac88ef_reading_speed_fae94e_comprehension_93217a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Question checks illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/increasing_re_852ca6_reading_speed_ac88ef_reading_speed_fae94e_comprehension_93217a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/increasing_re_852ca6_reading_speed_ac88ef_reading_speed_fae94e_comprehension_93217a-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
The best comprehension questions are designed to expose [skimming]({{ 'skimming/' | relative_url }}). Instead of asking readers to retrieve a single detail, they require readers to connect information across the text, identify the main point, draw inferences, and explain how one idea leads to another. Research on reading comprehension consistently distinguishes between literal recall and deeper inferential understanding, with inference-making recognised as a central component of genuine comprehension.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nfer.ac.uk/media/1aafth05/edr01.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nfer.ac.uk">[NFER]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nfer.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NFEREffective teaching of inference skills for reading</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 8, 2009 — by A Kispal · 2008 · Cited by 126 — The ability to make inferences is, in simple terms, the ability to use two or more...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 8, 2009</span></span></span>

## Why factual recall is not enough

A common mistake in reading-speed tests is relying on questions that can be answered from one memorable sentence.

For example, after a 1,200-word article, a quiz might ask:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What year did the event occur?
* What was the name of the company?
* How many participants were involved?

</div>

A reader who skimmed strategically may answer all three correctly despite understanding very little of the passage as a whole.

Reading researchers often distinguish between literal comprehension and inferential comprehension. Literal questions test information explicitly stated in the text. Inferential questions require readers to combine information, recognise implications, and construct meaning beyond individual sentences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lexialearning.com/blog/3-types-of-reading-comprehension-compared-inferential-literal-and-evaluative" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lexialearning.com">[Lexia+2study.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lexialearning.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Lexia3 Types of Reading Comprehension Compared</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lexia9 Feb 2026 — Explore the three main levels of reading comprehension: literal, inferential, and evaluative. Develop instructional str...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because skimming tends to preserve fragments of information while weakening understanding of structure, causation, and argument. A quiz that relies heavily on factual recall can therefore overestimate comprehension and make a fast reading speed look more effective than it really is.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED243090.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ERICDifferences in Literal and Inferential Comphension</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In grade 2, there were two literal comprehension and three inferential comprehension questions. for...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Question types that test real understanding

The strongest reading-speed assessments mix several kinds of questions. Each targets a different way that superficial reading can fail.

### Main-idea questions

Main-idea questions reveal whether the reader understood the passage's central [purpose]({{ 'purpose/' | relative_url }}).

Examples:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What is the author's primary argument?
* Which statement best summarises the passage?
* What problem is the text mainly trying to explain?

</div>

These questions are difficult to answer correctly if the reader has only collected disconnected facts. Research and educational assessment frameworks consistently treat main-idea identification as a higher-value indicator of comprehension than simple detail recall.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hechingerreport.org/proof-points-reading-comprehension-main-idea/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hechingerreport.org">[The Hechinger Report+2Gallaudet University]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hechingerreport.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Hechinger Report What happens to reading comprehension when students</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Hechinger ReportWhat happens to reading comprehension when students...May 26, 2025 — 26 May 2025 — Some researchers say students nee...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 26, 2025</span></span></span>

A useful test design includes at least one question where several answer choices contain true details but only one captures the overall message.

### Cause-and-effect questions

Skimming often preserves events while losing the connections between them.

Questions such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Why did the policy change occur?
* What led to the outcome described in the final section?
* Which factor most influenced the result?

</div>

require readers to understand relationships rather than isolated information.

Readers who have merely scanned headings and keywords frequently recognise what happened but struggle to explain why it happened. Cause-and-effect questions exploit that weakness and are therefore effective filters against inflated speed scores.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED243090.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.eric.ed.gov">[ERIC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.eric.ed.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ERICDifferences in Literal and Inferential Comphension</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In grade 2, there were two literal comprehension and three inferential comprehension questions. for...Read more...</span></span></span>

### Inference questions

Inference questions are among the best tools for detecting shallow reading.

An inference requires the reader to combine two or more pieces of information to reach a conclusion that the text does not explicitly state. The National Foundation for Educational Research defines inferencing as using multiple pieces of information from a text to derive an implicit meaning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nfer.ac.uk/media/1aafth05/edr01.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nfer.ac.uk">[NFER]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nfer.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NFEREffective teaching of inference skills for reading</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 8, 2009 — by A Kispal · 2008 · Cited by 126 — The ability to make inferences is, in simple terms, the ability to use two or more...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 8, 2009</span></span></span>

Examples include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What can be inferred about the author's view?
* Why was the character likely concerned?
* Which conclusion follows most logically from the evidence presented?

</div>

These questions are powerful because a reader cannot answer them by remembering a single sentence. They require construction of a mental model of the text, something that extensive skimming often undermines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nfer.ac.uk/media/1aafth05/edr01.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nfer.ac.uk">[NFER+2Erasmus University Rotterdam]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nfer.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">NFEREffective teaching of inference skills for reading</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">October 8, 2009 — by A Kispal · 2008 · Cited by 126 — The ability to make inferences is, in simple terms, the ability to use two or more...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 8, 2009</span></span></span>

### Questions that connect distant parts of the passage

Many weak quizzes focus on information from one paragraph at a time.

A stronger approach asks readers to integrate information from multiple sections:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* How does the example in the introduction support the conclusion?
* Which earlier point best explains the final recommendation?
* What theme links the first and last sections?

</div>

Research on both human and machine reading-comprehension assessment shows that questions requiring reasoning across multiple sentences or sections are substantially harder to answer through superficial pattern matching alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09384" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv What Makes Reading Comprehension Questions Easier?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Makes Reading Comprehension Questions Easier?August 28, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 28, 2018</span></span></span>

These questions are particularly useful when evaluating reading speed because they test whether the reader retained the structure of the text rather than isolated fragments.

### Conclusion and implication questions

A reader who skims may recognise facts but miss what the facts mean.

Questions targeting conclusions and implications include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* What is the most reasonable takeaway from the passage?
* Which recommendation would the author most likely support?
* What broader implication follows from the evidence?

</div>

Such questions require synthesis rather than retrieval. They [measure]({{ 'measure/' | relative_url }}) whether the reader understood the destination of the argument, not just the route taken to reach it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.school-page.com/articles/balanced-question-types-factual-inferential-critical-thinking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: school-page.com">[School Pages]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">school-page.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Explore how AI can help generate diverse</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">School PagesBalanced Question Types: Factual, Inferential, and Critical...Learn to create comprehensive reading assessments using Factua...</span></span></span>

## Common quiz designs that fail to catch skimming

Several popular comprehension formats give readers too much credit.

**Single-detail questions.** A reader may answer correctly after noticing only [one sentence]({{ 'one-sentence/' | relative_url }}).

**Question order that follows the passage exactly.** This allows readers to rely on short-term memory of recent details rather than understanding.

**Overuse of names, dates, and numbers.** Such information is often easy to recognise even during rapid [scanning]({{ 'scanning-vs-reading/' | relative_url }}).

**Too few questions.** A three-question quiz provides little evidence of comprehension. One lucky guess can substantially distort results.

Many reading-rate studies and [speed-reading]({{ 'myths/' | relative_url }}) discussions use a comprehension threshold around 70–80 percent. The logic is straightforward: reading faster is valuable only if understanding remains high enough to make the reading useful. Research reviews discussing accelerated reading methods note that an 80 percent comprehension benchmark is often treated as acceptable, whereas substantially lower levels become increasingly risky for meaningful understanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/lnc3.12408" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Compass]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Compass Improving reading rates and comprehension?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Benefits and...Mar 17, 2021 — While an 80% threshold for comprehension accuracy may be considered appropriate, allowing comprehension to...</span></span></span>

For self-testing, a useful rule is:

* Below 70% comprehension: speed is probably too high for that material.
* Around 70–80%: acceptable for lighter reading, news, or familiar topics.
* Above 80%: strong evidence that comprehension survived the increased pace.

The exact threshold depends on the purpose. Reading a novel for enjoyment may tolerate some loss of detail. Reading a contract, research paper, or [technical]({{ 'technical-texts/' | relative_url }}) report usually requires a higher standard.

The key principle remains the same: a words-per-minute score should only count if comprehension questions demonstrate that the reader understood the text.
